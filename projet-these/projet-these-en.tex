\begin{abstract}
This project studies the interesting as a philosophical object. It
starts from an ordinary experience that is hard to describe: an idea emerges,
a passage holds our attention and makes us read or hear it again to understand
what stopped us. The central
hypothesis is simple: what is interesting, for a subject, is what triggers a
process of construction in that subject. The subject then begins to
distinguish, question, connect, model, test, or produce differently. The
interesting names neither an intrinsic quality of the form nor an additional
mental state, but the relational power of an encounter. The neologism
\emph{interescence} will name the dynamic thereby triggered, from the break with
indifference to the moment when the subject abandons the process, begins constructing
again, or passes on what has been constructed. The
thesis will reconstruct its genealogy, identify its properties, and study it
in five fields: music, artificial intelligence, creativity, philosophy, and
literature. Its constructive core will be situated as a direct continuation of
Christian Garve, without attributing to him the developments proposed here: the
enigma, controllable grips, constructive peeling, recursion, and the social
regimes of difficulty. A project for a complete French translation of
his essay, undertaken with Christian Berner for Vrin, will accompany this
research. The limited place given to this theory in the philosophical tradition
will be explained through the demotion of \emph{Popularphilosophie}, its
aesthetic, Romantic, and moral reinterpretations, and its later dispersion
among the sciences of the psyche.
Its reflexive dimension will be decisive: the concept of the interesting can
itself become interesting and transform the subject attempting to grasp it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Origin, Question, and Aim}

This work emerges from a constructivist and empirical practice of artificial
intelligence research. I have spent forty years building systems that generate,
transform, or accompany music and text. The doctoral project is not intended to
summarise this work or retrospectively impose an artificial philosophical unity
on it. It starts from a personal question: \emph{what am I doing} when I
build a system, recognise a solution, reject an output that is correct but
uninteresting, or stop before a form that seems to impose itself?

The guiding question is:

\begin{quote}
Under what conditions does an object trigger a process of construction in a
subject? How does this process continue, become exhausted, or change?
\end{quote}

A song, a proof, a sentence, or an idea may hold attention disproportionately
and provoke comparisons, make us read or hear it again, and lead us to try
variations. This effect cannot be
reduced to raw novelty, because randomness surprises without necessarily being
interesting; nor to beauty, because a familiar form may remain beautiful while
becoming predictable; nor to popularity; nor to subjective preference
understood as a datum without a history.

The work will proceed in three stages. It will first locate the interesting
among neighbouring notions. It will then define it precisely enough to
distinguish cases and construct counterexamples. Finally, it will show what the
concept helps us understand in actual practices, without
automatically turning a scientific result or a personal experience into
philosophical proof.

\part{Establishing the Interesting as a Philosophical Problem}

\section{A Word Already Divided}

The adjective \emph{interesting} belongs to a lexical family whose ambivalence
precedes any philosophical elaboration. From the thirteenth century onwards,
French \emph{intérêt} adapted the Latin \emph{interest}, an impersonal form of
\emph{interesse}: ``it matters,'' but also, more literally, ``there is a
difference.'' The medieval substantivisation of \emph{interesse} notably
designated compensation due for harm or breach of contract; this history leads
to the financial sense of the return on money lent
\parencite{academie2026interet,cnrtl2026interet}. \emph{Intéresser} and
\emph{intéressant} therefore do not derive from a pure vocabulary of curiosity:
they also inherit the senses of what matters to someone, concerns them, or
engages their advantage.

Everyday French keeps these regimes together. A French speaker may \emph{porter
intérêt} to a question, \emph{avoir intérêt} in acting, defend \emph{ses
intérêts}, lend money \emph{à intérêt}, or call someone \emph{intéressée}, a
word that hesitates between attentive engagement and the pursuit of personal gain.
Conversely, \emph{désintéressé} is morphologically negative but often morally
laudatory. These are not accidental homonyms: in each case, something counts
for a subject, but this ``counting'' may designate an attentional grip, a
practical stake, an advantage, or a monetary value. A theory of the interesting
must consequently specify which kind of interest it mobilises instead of
allowing these meanings to circulate silently.

English \emph{interesting} adds a pragmatic ambiguity to this polysemy. Taken
literally, the word attributes to its object the power to hold attention. In an
exchange, however, \emph{That's interesting} may, depending on prosody and
context, mark surprise or perplexity, suspend assent, mitigate disagreement, or
even politely close a conversation that has become boring. The Anglophone
tradition of understatement, particularly associated with British English,
thus allows an apparently positive utterance to communicate reservation or
indirect denial \parencite[29--30]{wierzbicka2006english}. This use does not
constitute one more stable lexical meaning: it shows precisely that calling
something \emph{interesting} does not prove that the activity characteristic of
interest has actually begun.

\section{A Blind Spot Rather Than an Absence}

Philosophy has not simply ignored the interesting. It has addressed several of
its dimensions under other names: access to the intelligible in Plato and
Aristotle
\parencite{platon2016republique,platon1995theetete,aristote1991metaphysique,aristote2005seconds};
intuition and evidence in Descartes and Spinoza
\parencite{descartes2002regles,descartes1992meditations,spinoza1966ethique};
judgement in Kant \parencite{kant2001raison,kant1993juger}; invention and the
reconfiguration of problems in Peirce, Poincaré, Hadamard, Bachelard, and Valéry
\parencite{peirce1955writings,poincare1908science,hadamard1959invention,bachelard1999formation,valery1973cahiers};
memory, duration, and the temporal constitution of meaning in Bergson, Husserl,
and Merleau-Ponty
\parencite{bergson2013evolution,bergson2013pensee,husserl1970experience,husserl1957logique,merleau1945phenomenologie};
aspect change in Wittgenstein \parencite{wittgenstein2004recherches}; and
symbolisation, difference, and repetition in Goodman and Deleuze
\parencite{goodman1976languages,deleuze1968difference}.

The proposed diagnosis is therefore not one of lexical absence but of
\emph{functional dispersion}. Truth evaluates the validity of a proposition;
beauty, a particular regime of aesthetic judgement; pleasure, a tone of
experience; surprise, a departure from expectation; curiosity, an orientation
towards answering a determinate question; relevance, a relation to a question or goal; and
preference, an ordering of choices. Each concept captures a genuine dimension.
None explains by itself why a correct, intelligible, true, or pleasant form
triggers an activity in a subject that transforms their representations and
possibilities for action.

This last formulation must not erase a decisive historical counterexample.
Garve does not merely approach the interesting under another name: in 1779 he
offers an explicit theory of attention, gaps, and the activity of
representations. The blind spot therefore concerns less the existence of the
mechanism than its limited reception and the absence of sustained discussion
that would have developed its consequences.

The interesting may name this level of selection and transformation. It does
not replace neighbouring notions: it allows us to examine their articulations
and divergences. One can like a piece of music without finding it interesting,
be surprised without gaining a new grip, recognise a proposition as true
without judging it worth pursuing, or be mobilised by an object that is painful
and morally objectionable.

\section{State of the Art and Reading Programme}

\subsection{Garve: A Direct Antecedent of the Constructive Hypothesis}

A direct reading of Christian Garve's essay \emph{Einige Gedanken über das
Interessirende} establishes a first result of the genealogy
\parencite[253--274, 313--317]{garve1779interessirende}. The interesting names
the way an object fixes attention without voluntary effort, through a gentle
attraction connected with pleasure and with the relation between the object and
the subject's prior state. This attention is prospective: it is already
directed towards the continuation of the series and remains active while its
outcome retains some obscurity.

Garve goes beyond an economy of attention. An explanation becomes interesting
only if the subject has previously experienced, however obscurely, the
difficulty it resolves. The new idea illuminates thoughts already possessed but
still incomplete, repairs a lack encountered in an operation, or is constructed
from materials previously present. When the outcome becomes certain, activity
diminishes. A perceived gap, dependence on the subject's history, desired
continuation, construction, and local self-extinction thus form a core strikingly
close to the central hypothesis of this thesis.

The historical reconstruction by \textcite{nannini2018garve} and the lexicon by
\textcite{nannini2018interesting} guided this return to the primary text without
replacing it. The constructive definition will therefore not be presented as a
discovery \emph{ex nihilo}. Its contribution must lie in systematising the
mechanism, defining controllable grips, distinguishing gap, enigma, problem, and
constraint, analysing exhaustion and renewal, generalising from reception to
creation, and making its recursion explicit.

This work will also lead to an editorial project undertaken with Christian
Berner for Vrin: a complete French translation of Garve's essay,
accompanied by an introduction and notes. Translating the text is part of the
research itself, since the vocabulary of interest, attention, gaps, and mental
activity bears directly on the interpretation of the mechanism.

\subsection{Why Did Garve Not Inspire a School of Thought?}

The relative neglect of Garve's essay does not demonstrate the philosophical
sterility of its object. It instead calls for the study of a failed programme:
the empirical, public, and essayistic philosophy that had made the question
formulable lost its authority, while the functions of the interesting were
captured and redistributed. The object may have survived without a common
concept, and the word without preserving the unity of its object.

A first mechanism is the defeat of \emph{Popularphilosophie}. Garve regarded
communication with the public and philosophy's usefulness as intellectual
requirements; these values were progressively excluded by a culture that
opposed serious philosophical work to public accessibility
\parencite{thompson2018garve}. His descriptive approach to life and action thus
remained in the shadow of Kantian transcendental philosophy
\parencite{bachmannmedick2008anziehungskraft}. A changing relation between an
object, a subject's history, and an emerging activity is better studied through
essays, cases, and local distinctions than through a grand system.

A second mechanism is a double aesthetic capture. The Kantian problematic draws
the vocabulary of interest towards disinterestedness and the purity of the
judgement of taste \parencite{kant1993juger}. Schlegel then promotes the
interesting as a feature of modern, individual, unfinished, and perfectible
poetry, in contrast to the objective beauty of ancient poetry
\parencite{schlegel2001study,nannini2018interesting}. These shifts preserve
genuine dimensions --- the subject's participation, expectation, the desire for
continuation, and historicity --- but tend to cover over Garve's more general
question: how does a representation cease to be indifferent and set the subject
in motion? Aesthetics offers a useful field of study, but it does not exhaust
the question of the interesting.

A third capture is moral. Garve considers which passions it would be useful to
arouse and poetry's formative power, yet his 1779 appendix explicitly separates
this question from the inquiry into the nature of the interesting. The thesis
must therefore distinguish the descriptive genesis of attention, its modulation
by approval and sympathy, and the normative destination a society wishes to give
it. This separation makes it possible to describe the phenomenon without
deciding its value in advance, while preserving the responsibility of those who
organise attention.

Moving beyond aesthetics leads to a more fundamental function of the psyche.
The hierarchy of relative prepotency in \textcite{maslow1943motivation} shows
that a dominant need can reorganise perception, memory, and intelligence until
other interests disappear, and that its satisfaction releases new motivations.
The interesting will not be added as another level of the pyramid. It instead
describes how something temporarily gains priority and calls for an activity to
continue. Boredom
symmetrically signals the psyche's difficulty in finding a grip that warrants
continuation. Maslow did not, moreover, draw the pyramid commonly attributed to
him; that later visualisation rigidifies a theory that allowed overlaps,
exceptions, and reversals \parencite{bridgman2019maslow}.

Finally, disciplinary progress may have dispersed the mechanism among attention,
curiosity, motivation, learning, boredom, suspense, preference, and engagement.
This specialisation is not in itself an impoverishment; it becomes a problem
when each division obscures the complete temporal relation. The analogy with
the premature mathematisation described by \textcite{russell1995rationality} is
methodological: increased precision can lead results away from the original
problem. The oral formulation according to which the interesting parts were
``defined away,'' transcribed by \textcite{pachet1997representation}, does not
appear in the published paper and establishes no historical lineage between
Russell and Garve. It warns the present thesis against a theory that succeeds in
defining a magnitude only after removing the phenomenon it sought to explain.

Several causes may therefore explain this neglect. The research must establish
them, reconstruct Garve's reception, and follow the shift
from \emph{das Interessierende} to \emph{das Interessante}, compare histories of
aesthetics, and map the migration of the problem into the sciences of the
psyche. For each proposed cause, evidence of reception must be distinguished
from the thesis's retrospective reconstructions.

\subsection{A Theory That Was Rarely Taken Up}

The genealogy must still examine the opposition between the interesting and
beauty and its relation to modernity in Friedrich Schlegel; Schopenhauer's essay
\emph{Ueber das Interessante}; the relation between boredom, variation, and
\emph{det Interessante} in Kierkegaard; and the articulation of the interesting
and truth in Whitehead.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, a small number of works have
directly attempted to conceptualise the interesting. Stace, Kolnai, and Ngai
have analysed its aesthetic value, apparent weakness, and discursive circulation
\parencite{stace1944interestingness,kolnai1964concept,ngai2008merely,ngai2012categories}.
\textcite{epstein2009interesting} stresses its transdisciplinary structure, and
\textcite{grimm2011interesting} its epistemic value in selecting questions.
These works prevent any premature claim to have discovered an untouched
continent. They show instead that the interesting is present, but that these
works remain scattered, without a common theory comparable to those of truth or beauty.

A contemporary article now provides a more direct interlocutor.
\textcite{vazard2026inquiry} distinguishes curiosity, directed at a question
whose answer structures inquiry, from interest, directed at an object, fact, or
domain through sustained, capacious, exploratory attention. Interest can thus
precede the questions it helps bring forth. Vazard further analyses it as a
feeling of value: the object is experienced, fittingly or unfittingly, as
meriting such attention. This proposal confirms the prospective function of the
interesting while correcting an overly goal-directed constructive definition:
at the outset, the subject may not yet know which aspect to explore or which
grip to seek.

Not all sources will have the same status. A verified bibliographic record, an
identified passage, a work actually read, and an interpretation integrated into
the argument represent different levels of knowledge. Sources already read may
support the text; the others form an
explicit programme of reading and verification. This distinction prevents a
bibliographic suggestion from being treated as an established authority.

\section{The Risk of Target Substitution}

Science has powerful instruments for making a problem tractable: operational
definition, decomposition, control of variables, repeatability, normalisation,
averaging, benchmarking, and optimisation. Yet these instruments also tend
silently to replace the original problem with a more stable substitute.
``Producing something interesting'' then becomes producing something
syntactically correct, semantically coherent, probable in a corpus, surprising,
or compliant with constraints.

There is therefore a \emph{risk of target substitution}. Researchers need not
have hostile intentions, and the argument is not anti-scientific. The difficulty
follows from the relational, historical, and
transformative nature of the phenomenon: making it measurable may neutralise
the relation, history, and transformation that constitute it when the indicator
is treated as a complete definition of the target. The warning against premature
mathematisation
\parencite{russell1995rationality,russell1995awardlecture} is a guiding case.

Each measure must therefore be treated as a local hypothesis. A successful
formalisation must not merely make the problem solvable; it must state what it
preserves from the question and what it abandons. The thesis will thus seek a
reflexive formalisation capable of retaining its residuals instead of declaring
them unimportant.

Substitution may also occur after the target has been correctly formalised.
\emph{Hidden Biases in Conditioning Autoregressive Models} distinguishes the
exact conditional distribution from the distribution actually produced by a
tractable procedure \parencite{pachet2026biases}. In the general class studied,
exact MAP inference is NP-hard and exact conditional normalisation is
\#P-hard; greedy, top-k, top-p, and beam search may also lose valid solutions.
Target, indicator, and procedure must therefore be kept separate: pressure for
tractability and operational approximation may change weights or support even
before this divergence is forgotten.

Tonal parsimony provides a complementary positive case
\parencite{pachet2026tonalparsimony}. It first minimises changes of key, then the
number of distinct centres, yet retains two anti-compression cases in which a
more compact analysis erases a minor excursion or intentionally distinct local
tonics. The residual is not declared external to the problem: it indicates
where the objective or local model must be enriched. Reflexive formalisation
does not abandon computation; it keeps visible the point where local success
ceases to preserve the relevant difference.

\part{Constructing the Concept}

\section{A Relational and Constructive Definition}

The core of the thesis can be stated without placing its consequences inside
its definition:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{What is interesting, for a subject, is what triggers a process of
construction in that subject.}
\end{quote}

Construction does not necessarily mean making a material object. A subject
constructs when they alter how they perceive, question, understand, or act:
they draw a distinction, formulate a question, connect elements, develop a
hypothesis or model, try a variation, produce a form, or invent an operation.
Merely executing an already mastered procedure is not enough. The encounter
must transform, even locally, the activity under way or its possibilities of
continuation.

This definition concerns the trigger, not success. The process may be brief,
fail, go astray, exhaust its object, or lead to a new grip. Its result should
therefore not be reintroduced into the definition as a vague promise. The
inquiry must instead distinguish fruitful, controllable, or transmissible
constructions from fascinations that produce no new capacity.

The interesting remains relational. The same form may trigger construction in
one subject and leave another indifferent, or lose this power for the same
subject after learning. The minimal relation may be written
\[
  I(F,S\mid H,t),
\]
where \(F\) is the form encountered, \(S\) the subject, \(H\) the social,
cultural, and technical horizon, and \(t\) the moment of encounter. This
notation does not complicate the definition; it identifies the variables on
which the triggering power depends. Attention, curiosity, and emotions are
possible manifestations of the process in the subject, not synonyms for the
interesting.

\subsection{From Object to Reification}

The word \emph{object} does not refer only to a material thing. It may refer to
what presents itself to a subject and becomes material for perception,
questioning, or action. Its etymology retains this image: the Scholastic Latin
\emph{objectum} means ``what is placed before''\footnote{CNRTL,
\href{https://www.cnrtl.fr/etymologie/objet}{etymology of \emph{objet}}.}.
The Greek \emph{problēma}, from which \emph{problem} derives, likewise names
what lies before us and comes from a verb that means, among other things, ``to
throw before''\footnote{CNRTL,
\href{https://www.cnrtl.fr/etymologie/probleme}{etymology of
\emph{problème}}.}. The two words do not share the same root, but they share an
image: something is put before the subject and may call for a response.

This leads to a new hypothesis: the interesting may lie at the root of
reification. What becomes interesting stands out from its background. The
subject notices it, returns to it, attributes properties to it, and begins to
construct with it. Something thereby becomes an object for that subject.
Reification begins when this relational birth is forgotten and the object
appears to exist by itself. Objectivation, which makes something identifiable
and workable, must therefore be distinguished from reification, which erases
the history of that construction \parencite{pachet2004reification}.

This hypothesis does not apply to every reification. An administration, a
market, a classification, or a program may impose objects without any prior
interest on the part of those affected. It first concerns lived reification:
what becomes interesting to a subject may become an object for that subject,
then appear independent of the relation that brought it into being.
Object-oriented programming offers an analogy drawn from the practice of
building systems, not a historical lineage.

\subsection{The Neologism ``Interescence''}

I call \emph{interescence} the relational and temporal process triggered when
something becomes interesting to a subject: a break with indifference, first
distinctions, construction, successive transformations, then abandonment,
new construction, or transmission. The distinction is therefore clear: \emph{the
interesting} qualifies the encounter's triggering power; \emph{interescence}
names the dynamic it sets in motion.

The term also makes it possible to speak of a field of interescence, its
displacement, and its passage from one subject to another. The adjective
\emph{interescential} refers to the analysis of these processes. These
neologisms remain proposed tools, not established results. They will be retained
only if they distinguish trigger, trajectory, and transfer more precisely than
ordinary uses of ``interest'' do.

\section{Candidate Properties}

The constructive definition remains a working hypothesis. The properties below
are neither all independent nor necessary in every case. They will help compare
cases, vary examples, and formulate objections.

These properties do not all have the same historical status. Relational
indexicality, path dependence, representational productivity, structured
incompleteness, prospective orientation, and weakening through certainty have a
direct antecedent in Garve. The specification of controllable grips,
constructive peeling, the distinction between consumable and generative
enigmas, creative generalisation, and self-application are the extensions tested
by the thesis. The possible priority of interest over the question has its
direct contemporary antecedent in Vazard.

\subsection{Relational and Dispositional Status}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Relational indexicality.} The interesting is neither contained
        in the form alone nor reducible to a private state. It varies with
        \(F\), \(S\), \(H\), and \(t\).
  \item \textbf{Passage into activity.} The interesting does not name a mental
        state. It explains the possible passage from indifference to attention,
        and then to curiosity, emotion, or an activity that changes the subject.
        These states show that the relation is active without defining it alone.
  \item \textbf{Possibility of a trigger.} A form may be capable of triggering a
        construction without actually doing so, for lack of attention, time, or
        competence. The disposition is actualised only under certain conditions
        of encounter.
  \item \textbf{Path dependence.} The order of experiences matters. A form
        encountered too early may remain opaque; after a new grip has been
        acquired, it may become interesting.
  \item \textbf{Possible priority over the question.} Interest may sustain
        open attention before the subject knows under which aspect to describe
        the object. The question and direction of inquiry can be products of
        interest rather than its prior conditions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dynamics of Construction}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \textbf{Assimilable gap.} There must be enough difference to call for a
        construction and enough grip for it to begin. The familiar tends
        towards triviality; the absolutely foreign towards noise or
        abandonment.
  \item \textbf{Production of ideas.} An interesting form generates
        more comparisons, hypotheses, questions, scenarios, or distinctions
        than it explicitly states.
  \item \textbf{Structured incompleteness.} A gap is interesting only if it
        calls for a directed continuation. The object provides clues for
        constructing what is missing or reformulating the question.
  \item \textbf{Transformation of the space of possibilities.} A strong
        construction does not merely add a representation: it changes the
        questions that can be asked, the distinctions available, or the actions
        that can be conceived.
  \item \textbf{Constructive peeling.} The trigger opens a succession of
        grips: each dispels an opacity, reveals a layer, or reformulates the
        problem, then becomes an instrument for the next operation. The process
        ends through exhaustion of the object, exhaustion of the subject,
        abandonment before a resistance that affords no grip, the deliberate
        closure of a still-generative construction, or renewal through
        productive resistance.
  \item \textbf{Non-monotonicity.} More information, surprise, or attention does
        not imply more interest. An explanation may renew the construction or
        close too quickly the indeterminacy that sustained it.
  \item \textbf{Self-extinction or renewal.} Some relations are consumable:
        resolution removes their interest. Others are generative: each new grip
        reveals a resistance or a further question.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Difficultuosity and Chalepodoxy}

The manuscript of \emph{La virtuosité à la portée des caniches}, forthcoming
from Hermann in 2027, distinguishes three regimes that must be kept separate
\parencite[manuscript, 13--16]{pachet2027virtuosite}. So-called intrinsic
difficulty attributes resistance to the task itself. \emph{Difficultuosity}, by
contrast, designates felt difficulty: a mobile relation between a subject,
their abilities, and a performance. It does not increase indefinitely with
complexity. In virtuosity, it culminates in a narrow zone in which an
achievement appears extreme yet still humanly credible; beyond that threshold,
the performance becomes an artefact and no longer elicits admiration in the
same way.

Transposed to the interesting, this notion specifies the practicable resistance
of an enigma. A difficulty that is too weak has already been resolved; a
difficulty that affords no grip promises no construction. This transposition
remains a hypothesis: difficultuosity first qualifies the reception of a
performance and must not become a general synonym for the interesting.

\emph{Chalepodoxy} describes a third case, not a higher degree of
difficultuosity. It relates a reputedly difficult domain, a subject who has not
overcome its resistance, and a third party whom that subject wishes to impress.
Difficulty then becomes symbolic capital: fluency, jargon, or confidence may
simulate mastery and depth without producing a new grip. It therefore provides
a diagnosis of a false positive:
\[
  \text{worked-through difficulty}
  \neq \text{felt difficulty}
  \neq \text{the social reputation of difficulty}.
\]
One cannot therefore infer a genuine construction from the duration of
attention, the opacity of a discourse, or the prestige attached to its
difficulty.

This dynamic opposes the interesting to contemplation in the strong sense used
here. The distinction is not between bodily activity and immobility: listening,
reading, or looking may involve intense constructive work. Contemplation names
the regime that suspends transformative operations in order to keep the object
intact or at a distance; it thereby forbids or postpones construction. As soon
as it produces a reusable distinction, variation, or new grip, it has already
shifted into the regime of the interesting.

This life of the interesting meets the creative process and its problem of
ending \parencite{pachet2026impossibilite}. Local decisions do not necessarily
contain their own stopping criterion. Completion may therefore require imposing
a limit on a construction that could continue. This closure changes how the
whole process is understood: the end of the construction does not
necessarily mean the death of interest or the exhaustion of the object.

\subsection{Value, Transfer, and Circulation}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \textbf{Relative affective neutrality.} The interesting may be
        pleasant, painful, disturbing, or repellent. It does not in itself
        imply moral or aesthetic approval.
  \item \textbf{Transfer to other fields.} A construction gains scope when it
        becomes a model or controlled metaphor for other domains.
  \item \textbf{Fruitful concision.} Some forms retrospectively condense
        several phenomena while prospectively opening new derivations.
  \item \textbf{Mediated contagion.} Interest is not transmitted as a property
        attached to an object. Yet one may prepare the conditions for its
        appearance in others by establishing an expectation or providing
        grips.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Can everything be made interesting?}

This question directly extends the constructive account. Making a subject
interesting does not mean depositing a ready-made interest in another person's
mind. It means accompanying the same mental work through which the interesting
is constituted: beginning to see problems, differences, and questions where
one previously saw only an indifferent subject matter. Such guidance requires
ingenuity in the engineer's sense of constructing. The teacher constructs the
conditions for a construction that the student must carry out.

Gilles de La Ménardière recalled with emotion how, against all expectations,
his teacher had eventually made administrative law interesting to him. Having
become a member of the French Council of State, he in turn succeeded in sharing
his enthusiasm for the difficult problem of respect for the law. This anecdote
does not prove that everything can be made interesting. It provides a case and
a criterion: guidance succeeds when interest outlasts the teacher's presence,
when the student pursues the newly discovered questions independently, and when
the student can in turn help others discover them. It thus requires us to
distinguish an autonomous construction from mere seduction by a mediator.

We must also distinguish the cost of exploration from the repetitive
exploitation of already profitable forms. If interesting encounters
are rare and imperfectly predictable, open production may expose many
unsuccessful attempts; this justifies neither a reduction in effective diversity
nor a concentration of exposure. To speak of manipulation, we must identify an
action, show its effect, and explain why the relation is unequal. The strong
statistical hypothesis about rarity remains a possible line of inquiry, not a
starting point.

\subsection{Reflexive Property}

\begin{enumerate}[resume]
  \item \textbf{Self-application.} The concept of the interesting may itself
        trigger the comparisons, distinctions, models, and revisions it
        describes. ``The interesting is interesting'' claims that the concept
        may, under certain conditions, enter its own domain of application.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Temporality, Learning, and the Almost Learnable}

Interest moves with learning. A pattern that is initially opaque may become
accessible, produce a gain in understanding, and then lose its force once
acquired. Theories of compression progress and intrinsic motivation formalise
one version of this dynamic
\parencite{schmidhuber1997interesting,schmidhuber2009compression,oudeyer2007intrinsic,oudeyer2007typology}.
If \(E_t(o,S)\) is the error of the model held by subject \(S\) when facing
object \(o\), local progress may be written
\[
  P_t(o,S)=E_{t-\Delta}(o,S)-E_t(o,S).
\]
Situations already mastered and situations that cannot be learned both have
progress close to zero. Progress niches are those in which improvement is
possible.

This temporal derivative must be distinguished from static compression of a
representation. Tonal parsimony may produce a globally compact analysis by
reducing its vocabulary of centres without measuring whether a subject has just
constructed that analysis, already possessed it, or will never hear it
\parencite{pachet2026tonalparsimony}. A low compactness value may be the grip
acquired through learning; it is neither the progress of that learning nor a
measure of the interesting.

This hypothesis connects with, but is not reducible to, the intermediate zone
between boredom and anxiety. With fixed skills, increasing difficulty yields
the sequence
\[
  \text{boredom}\longrightarrow\text{interesting/flow}
  \longrightarrow\text{anxiety}.
\]
For a fixed task, learning may reverse the path. The flow zone refers here to a
structural adjustment between competence and difficulty, not only to the
psychological state of absorption. Hesitation or rupture can remain interesting
as long as the subject retains a grip and can reorganise expectations.

Language shifts this frontier. A word, concept, or notation compresses
distinctions and sometimes operations into a reusable unit. What once required
work becomes a grip for a new construction. The zone of the almost learnable is
therefore historically mobile and symbolically mediated
\parencite{vygotsky1978mind,lenat1991thresholds,lenat2008turtle,koestler1989act,steels2005coordinating,steels2015talkingheads}.
Naming alone is insufficient: a term is a grip only if it can be unfolded into
distinctions, comparisons, predictions, operations, or controlled examples.

\section{Recursion: ``The Interesting Is Interesting''}

The relation first possesses a diachronic recursion:
\[
  I(F,S\mid H,t)\longrightarrow
  \text{construction}\longrightarrow S'
  \longrightarrow I(F,S'\mid H,t+1).
\]
Construction modifies the subject and therefore the conditions of later
evaluations. The object is no longer encountered by the same subject in the
relevant sense: their categories, expectations, and space of possibilities
have changed.

It can also apply to the concept itself. It may become interesting to
understand why something is interesting, and then to understand what this first
explanation still leaves unexplained. The inquiry then takes as its new object
the relation that triggered it. The formula \emph{the interesting is
interesting} is therefore not a universal tautology, but a claim of possible
self-application.

This loop explains why the concept is difficult to fix. Every acquired grip
changes the repertoire of examples, capacities for discrimination, and frontier
of the problem; a productive definition thus brings forth the cases that will
require its revision. Neither paradox nor ineffability follows. Local and
controllable properties can still be formulated, but the theory must account
for its own effect, its history, and its revisions.

Finally, self-application is not a criterion of truth. An interesting theory of
the interesting may be false; a boring theory may be accurate. Recursion
describes what the inquiry does to its object and its subject, not what validates
its propositions.

\section{Theoretical Framework and Natural Explanation}

The theory must answer three questions. How do the subject's history and
judgement change the experience? How do memory and expectation give shape to
lived time? How does an organisation as a whole produce effects that its
isolated elements do not explain? The philosophy of the subject, the
phenomenology of time, and the study of complex systems offer answers to compare
\parencite{petitot1993phenomenologie,petitot2002naturaliser}.

The preface to Part III of the \emph{Ethics} provides a precedent
\parencite{spinoza1966ethique}. Spinoza refuses to
treat human affects as an ``empire within an empire'' exempt from the common
laws of nature. His example invites us to seek the causes of a subjective
experience without turning it into a property of the object. Its conditions
and development can be compared without locating interest in the form alone.

Ideas of affections involve both ``the nature of our body and the present nature
of the external body''
\parencite[III, prop. XXVII, dem.]{spinoza1861oeuvres}. This sentence reminds us
that the effect of an encounter depends on the form, the subject's history and
present state, the social setting, and the circumstances. The interesting then
changes what the subject can perceive, understand, anticipate, or do.

Explaining this relation will require comparison between subjects' accounts,
their behaviour, their learning, the forms under study, and computational
models. None of these viewpoints will be sufficient on its own. The pleasures
of discovery and construction
\parencite{feynman1999pleasure,florman1996existential} may accompany the
interesting without defining it: painful understanding may remain interesting,
whereas a pleasing explanation with no discriminating power may be only an
impression of clarity.

\part{The Concept at Work}

\section{Music: Temporality and Micro-emotions}

Music will be the main field of study because it makes relations
between memory, expectation, surprise, resolution, repetition, and exhaustion
especially visible. Meyer and later Narmour showed that musical affect and
meaning depend on learned expectations that are fulfilled, delayed, or thwarted
\parencite{meyer1956emotion,narmour1990basic,narmour1992complexity}.
Information-dynamic and neuro-symbolic models specify some of these processes
\parencite{abdallah2009information,berger1999expectations,gang1999unified}.

Explaining why a track is interesting cannot, however, amount to locating an
average quantity of surprise. The inquiry will examine trajectories of
attention at several scales: local micro-emotions, relations between melody and
harmony, global form, listening history, and the listener's own activity.
Jotney songs will provide a guiding case: they may be heard as solutions to the
problem of sustaining an autonomous, singable melody while moving the harmony
without displaying the procedure.

\emph{Histoire d'une oreille} provides a phenomenological vocabulary and a
method \parencite{pachet2018oreille}. \emph{Superimposed listening} submits
music to internal variations. An \emph{optale} is the feeling that a form is a
local optimum because nearby variants fail; a \emph{pseudoptale} warns that
familiarity may simulate this necessity. \emph{False evidence} and
\emph{overattentive listening} make it possible to examine false positives
produced by listeners themselves. Music will thus test the temporality, path
dependence, assimilable gap, non-monotonicity, and self-extinction of interest.

Tonal parsimony adds a controllable operation of reconstruction
\parencite{pachet2026tonalparsimony}. A chord sequence admits several paths of
tonal centres: the analysis first chooses the most continuous transitions and
then the most compact global vocabulary. This economy becomes a grip when it
supports anticipation, variation, or intervention. Anti-compression cases then
act as counter-tests: a gain in representational unity no longer counts as
progress when it erases a functional difference for the ear or expert practice.

The good crossword clue described by Perec provides a linguistic analogue of
the optale \parencite{perec1999motscroises}. Its answer appears obvious once it
has been found, however insoluble the problem seemed before: the clue--answer pair then
appears as a local optimum that a more explicit formulation or another answer
would weaken. The case is crucial---even ``cruciverbal''---because it makes the
transformation from a plurality of readings to retrospective necessity
especially brief and observable.

\section{Artificial Intelligence: Explication and Loss of the Target}

Artificial-intelligence systems will provide a second field of study. Work on Flow
Machines, reflexive interactions, Markov generation under constraints, and
computational interestingness has produced objects, protocols, successes, and
failures
\parencite{pachet1999surprising,pachet1999active,pachet1999programs,pachet2000melodie,
pachet2002continuatorinteraction,pachet2004flowmachines,pachet2004reification,
pachet2004cybernetic,pachet2004childrenreflect,addessi2005experiments,
pachet2006clefs,pachet2006creativity,pachet2006reflective,pachet2008machines,
jones2009stimulating,pachet2006interactionsreflexives,addessi2006flowmachine,
aucouturier2002similarity,pachetroy2008hitsong,pachet2011hitsong,
lerch2011closedloop,
milipachet1995regularity,pachet2012virtuosity,martin2016creativeprocess,
pachet2021assisted,pachet2026markov}.
It makes explicit operations that are often left implicit: sampling,
constraint, evaluation, model construction, selection, and direction.

Their sequence is itself instructive. The initial programme isolates properties
of form and learned expectations; active listening introduces the subject's
path; flow machines construct a reflexive loop; and the analysis of reification
recalls that each model remains indexed to its nourishing examples and uses. The
thesis therefore does not retrospectively discover a unified doctrine in this
work; it reconstructs the relation that its differences left distributed.

An earlier antecedent already formulates a topology of the interesting within a
precise computational setting. To select what deserves paraphrase in a Cyc proof
trace, \textcite{milipachet1995regularity} distinguish three regimes: perfect
regularity, which is often tautological; arbitrary irregularity, which offers no
grip; and irregularity structured by the rules of the knowledge base, which may
reveal an important property of the world or a missing concept. This local
criterion for document generation is not yet a general psychological or
aesthetic theory. It nevertheless anticipates the central notion of an
explainable deviation, located between unsurprising order and unintelligible
novelty.

Two results sharpen this reconstruction directly. Studies of musical creativity
take the subject--system pair as their unit of analysis, comparing both its output
and the subject's experience with a condition without the system. The peer-feedback
composition experiment further shows that aesthetic agreement depends on the judges'
experience: aggregating ratings therefore does not remove the horizon from which they
are produced.

Reflexive systems add a distinction between imitation and interest. In
\emph{Des machines à sortir de soi}, interesting objects are described as
side-effects of interacting with an imperfect and continuously revised image of
the subject. Continuator sessions that best passed an immediate imitation test
were not necessarily those whose recordings later seemed most interesting
\parencite{pachet2008machines}. A local performance measure can therefore miss
the retrospective fertility of a trajectory.

Earlier work on similarity states an analogous limit even more directly. A
timbre measure can be valid while returning trivial associations; the Aha effect
appears when this proximity contradicts an expectation constructed from textual
metadata \parencite{aucouturier2002similarity}. An evaluation with ten users and
hand-set confidence levels prevent this from constituting a general theory. The
case nevertheless anticipates the relational thesis: the interesting is not
located in an isolated measure, but in what its discrepancy from another
expectation enables a subject to understand.

Work on \emph{Hit Song Science} provides an instructive negative result. Across
32,978 titles, audio descriptors and hundreds of human labels fail to classify
popularity above chance, while other subjective properties are learnable from
the same data \parencite{pachetroy2008hitsong}. The synthesis chapter explains
why the target is unstable: repeated exposure, social information, and broadcast
decisions help produce the success they are then used to predict
\parencite{pachet2011hitsong}. This does not prove that properties of form have
no effect; it prevents popularity from being treated as an intrinsic property
independent of its history.

Two canary experiments finally separate virtuosity from interaction. The
preceding chapter reports a study in which females prefer artificial trills that
combine bandwidth and rate beyond ordinary production limits: this supports a
sensitivity to gestural difficulty without demonstrating the motor resonance
hypothesised by \emph{difficultuosity}. \emph{De l'impossibilité de créer}
develops precisely this personal interpretation: because females possess a latent
vocal competence, they might evaluate the song relative to a possible body rather
than through an isolated acoustic measure \parencite{pachet2026impossibilite}.
This conceptual hypothesis must remain distinct from the experimental result. In
another study, females vocalise more
when the computer response is contingent than during playback of the same calls
(8.9 versus 3.13 vocalisations per minute among seven responding subjects)
\parencite{pachet2011hitsong,lerch2011closedloop}. The second result concerns the
active relation, not virtuosity; bringing them together shows two complementary
ways of experimentally constructing a situation in which preference or
engagement becomes observable.

An earlier milestone already formalises the interesting as a tension rather than a
scalar maximum. \textcite{pachet1999programs} reduce music selection to two
contradictory desires, repetition and surprise, then constructs programmes in which
similarity provides local continuity while difference and cardinality produce global
variety. The relevant value thus belongs to the trajectory: a title can become the
right piece at the right time without being preferred in isolation. The paper does not,
however, define a general measure of the interesting or an explicit constraint of
rupture. The thesis interprets variety as the possibility of breaking a continuity
that has become predictable and adds the subject's history, for whom the same sequence
may be too repetitive or too discontinuous.

The Flow Machines sequence provides another internal lesson. A probability of style
does not by itself produce long-range form; constraints make it possible to impose
metre, direction, variation, or the absence of copied fragments
\parencite{pachet2011markov,barbieri2012lyrics,papadopoulos2014avoiding,
papadopoulos2016nonplagiaristic,papadopoulos2016flowcomposer,
pachet2017variations}. Yet each guarantee remains relative to the property that has
been formalised. FlowComposer can thus produce a reharmonisation that is new, valid,
and stylistically recognisable, while still being described as less interesting than
the original. The difference between an admissible output and an interesting one is
therefore not an external objection: it appears within the construction of the systems
themselves.

The long version of \emph{Hidden Biases in Conditioning Autoregressive Models},
submitted to NeurIPS, radicalises this distinction
\parencite{pachet2026biases}. Satisfying the constraint guarantees neither the
correct weights among valid solutions nor preservation of their full support.
The paper does not provide a theory of the interesting; it provides technical
evidence that the stated formal problem and the generator's effective behaviour
may diverge under pressure for tractability.

This difficulty motivates an architectural test. A transformer can explore
hypotheses, reformulations, and decompositions in breadth; a SAT, SMT, or
constraint solver can pursue their consequences in depth and return solutions,
conflicts, or counterexamples. A hybrid loop would use this feedback to revise
the representation itself. The test will compare a transformer alone, a solver
alone, and the hybrid loop to determine whether cooperation merely learns to
choose better branches or invents grips that change the practical difficulty of
the problem. This architecture provides a hypothesis to test. It does not assume
that a software component directly corresponds to a human psychological mechanism.

Experiments with the Continuator and young children add a temporal qualification.
Sustained interaction need not progress linearly: some dead moments readjust the
dialogue, and a system error may be abandoned or reused as musical material depending
on the grip it leaves the subject. Attention duration is therefore insufficient; the
sequence of anticipations, ruptures, and resumptions must be reconstructed
\parencite{addessi2005experiments,addessi2006flowmachine}.

AI is also the privileged reflexive case of target substitution. A
system may solve the formal problem while removing its interesting part.
Metrics of novelty, surprise, diversity, preference, or popularity are local
indicators; they must not be confused with the relational target
\(I(F,S\mid H,t)\). Relevant experimental success will therefore not merely be
the generation of objects judged interesting, but the capacity of a model to
predict or explain transformations of attention, learning, and construction.

\section{Creativity: Producing, Judging, and Reconstructing a Problem}

Creativity makes it possible to distinguish the production of novelty from the
production of the interesting. An output may be unprecedented yet arbitrary; a
solution may use familiar materials while reconfiguring the space of
possibilities. Some forms appear necessary after they emerge even though they
could not have been deduced beforehand. They reveal their own constraints and
create an impression of surface simplicity that conceals an improbable genesis
\parencite{pachet2026impossibilite,pachet2027virtuosite}.

An interesting object may thus be encountered before its problem is known. The
subject reconstructs backwards the constraints satisfied by the solution, the
alternatives it excludes, and the question it makes visible. This reconstruction
must not become ad hoc: it should support variations, predictions, or
comparisons that could have failed. The study of creativity will mainly test the
production of ideas, structured incompleteness, transformation of the space of
possibilities, and fruitful concision.

\section{Philosophy: Fertility and Self-application}

Philosophical texts will help to clarify the concept, reveal its presuppositions,
and formulate counterexamples.
The historical corpus will show how the phenomenon has been distributed among
judgement, aesthetics, curiosity, attention, invention, and boredom.

Philosophy also makes it possible to study self-application. A theory of the interesting
must explain why it itself triggers distinctions and new questions, while accepting
that this productivity does not validate it. Its evaluation will therefore
require two sets of criteria: conceptual yield, production of problems, and
transfers on one hand; coherence, historical accuracy, and resistance to
objections on the other.

The \emph{Snarky} project provides a personal and testable case. Formalising the
whole of Part III of the \emph{Ethics} as second-order inference rules does not
in itself establish a new interpretation or guarantee a better understanding of
Spinoza. It does, however, require the dependencies among definitions, axioms,
and propositions to be reconstructed explicitly, thereby exposing choices of
representation that usually remain implicit. This work thus provides an active
way of making the text one's own. This participatory appropriation may be
compared with the IKEA effect, but as an analogy rather than empirical proof:
the established effect concerns the increased valuation of an object after
successful participation in its construction, not learning or interpretive
accuracy \parencite{norton2012ikea}. Its transposition to creative systems
similarly shifts the issue from the intrinsic quality of the result to the
quality of the interaction and the subject's ability to recognise their own
contribution in the object \parencite[152--153]{pachet2026generation}.

\section{Literature: Incompleteness, Style, and Interpretation}

Literature provides a field distinct from music because construction is
mediated by language, narrative, voices, and semantic expectations. It will
allow us to study how a sentence or narrative supplies enough grips to orient
interpretation without exhausting it. Interest may arise from structured
incompleteness, narrative expectation, formal constraint, a ruse with language,
or a displacement of ordinary categories.

This field must also distinguish fertility from pseudo-depth. Opacity may
generate interpretations because it is structured; it may also sustain only the
indefinite promise that a key will eventually arrive. Stylistic variations,
paraphrases, competing continuations, and comparisons between readings will
help determine whether a text produces transferable grips or merely
fascination. Literature will thus test structured incompleteness, mediated
contagion, dependence on the cultural horizon, and interpretive recursion.

An \emph{interescential} analysis will add a deeper question: not only why the
text is interesting, but what begins, continues, or ceases to matter to its
characters, narrator, and reader. Its unit will be the dynamic relation through
which an object, person, sign, or possibility opens new grips. This method must
never simply rename every motivation ``interescence'': pain, lack, obligation,
or a demand for repair may exert tremendous force without any object becoming
interesting to the character.

Three texts will provide an initial comparison. Proust will make it possible to
trace how interescences begin, change, become exhausted, or begin again;
\emph{Bartleby}, the apparent depletion of internal interescence and its
transfer to the narrator and reader; and the \emph{Book of Job}, extreme moral
and affective motivation without identifiable internal interescence. Job seeks
to state his case and obtain an answer, but the text does not show a field that
he explores because it has become interesting to him. The divine speeches on
creation lead to silence and retraction rather than represented curiosity: the
enigma becomes interesting to the reader, not necessarily to Job
\parencite{jps1985tanakh}.

This case finally opens a cautious genealogical hypothesis. Some religious
texts may represent psychic life as driven mainly by trial, law, or obligation,
whereas the modern novel may make more visible the singular relations through
which something comes to matter. Interescence would then take over an immanent
orienting function once performed by morality, without normatively replacing
morality. Kant provides a hinge by showing how the law becomes an incentive and
an interest for a finite being \parencite{kant1848moralepratique}. This sequence
remains to be tested: a genuine religious counter-case would have to present a
non-prescribed object that opens successive, transformative grips for a
character. Intense motivation alone is not enough.

\section{Comparative Method and Criteria of Testing}

The general discipline of the inquiry can be condensed into a maxim:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Conceptualise the interesting without making the concept indifferent to
the experience that gives rise to it.}
\end{quote}

This maxim organises two reversible moments. \emph{Fidelity to the phenomenon}
begins with encounters that have actually been experienced and describes the
break with indifference, the orientation of attention, resistance, grip, the
activity triggered, and then exhaustion or renewal. \emph{Extraction of
structure} compares the cases, produces distinctions and counterexamples, and
formulates revisable invariants. The concept must finally return to the cases
that motivated it in order to encounter its false positives, residues, and
conditions of revision.

Garve shows that an inquiry beginning from effective psychology can seek general
conditions; the Kantian demand prevents it from stopping at private impressions.
These two points provide a method without assuming a lineage or merging the two
doctrines.

The study will cover five fields. They will show what the concept actually
explains and where it falls short. The research will combine:

\begin{itemize}
  \item conceptual analysis of the interesting and its neighbours;
  \item historical reconstruction and direct reading of sources;
  \item a situated phenomenology of musical, literary, mathematical,
        scientific, and philosophical cases;
  \item counterfactual comparisons: internal variations, merely competent
        continuations, new readings or hearings, and failed imitations;
  \item reflexive analysis of constructed systems and, where relevant,
        experiments on returning to the object, abandonment, learning, and changing
        judgements;
  \item confrontation with models of expectation, compression, curiosity,
        flow, and constrained generation.
\end{itemize}

Three levels will remain distinct: scientific results and effective properties
of systems; philosophical propositions; and argued comparisons showing what
the construction of a system or the analysis of a case helps us understand
without claiming to prove it.

A hypothesis will gain strength if it can describe a trajectory, identify the
conditions under which interest varies, distinguish phenomena previously
confused, anticipate a change, or produce a counterexample. The thesis will also
accept negative results: some properties may hold only in one field, and no
single model will be assumed capable of exhausting the object.

\section{Corpus}

The main corpus will bring together \emph{Histoire d'une oreille}, Jotney songs,
the essays on the impossibility of creation and on virtuosity
\parencite{pachet2018oreille,pachet2026impossibilite,pachet2027virtuosite}, work
on generative systems and computational interestingness, and the philosophical
and scientific sources actually used.

The corpus will be organised in three circles: sources directly argued with in
the manuscript; cases for comparison and objection; and a programme of readings
still to be consolidated. The literary corpus will be narrowed according to the
properties to be tested rather than expanded in pursuit of historical
exhaustiveness. This organisation must maintain an explicit link between each
source, the proposition it supports, and the actual status of its reading.

\section{Expected Contributions}

The thesis aims to make six contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a problematised history that restores Garve as a direct antecedent of
        the constructive hypothesis and explains why this core was seldom taken
        up, through demotion, aesthetic and moral reinterpretations, and disciplinary
        dispersion;
  \item a relational, dispositional, and constructive definition, expressed by
        \(I(F,S\mid H,t)\), together with a hypothesis about the passage from
        the interesting to the constitution of an object and, sometimes, to
        its reification;
  \item an organised cluster of properties for comparing the appearance,
        maintenance, circulation, exhaustion, and renewal of interest;
  \item a reflexive theory explaining both the concept's self-application and
        part of its elusiveness;
  \item a series of differential tests in music, AI, creativity, philosophy,
        and literature;
  \item a method of local formalisation that explicitly distinguishes target,
        indicator, effective procedure, scientific result, and philosophical
        scope.
\end{enumerate}

The outcome will not necessarily be a closed definition. It may take the form
of a coherent set of properties, distinctions, and criteria of
variation. A formal extension will examine, in particular,
non-compositionality, non-monotonicity, dynamic modalities, and the conditions
of a minimal axiomatics.

        missing concept. This local criterion is an antecedent of the
        explainable deviation defended in the thesis.
  \item \textcite{pachet2012virtuosity} distributes virtuosity between an
        embodied Markov texture and an intentional score of decisions made at
        the level of musical beats. ``Markovian boredom'' and the transformations
        that break it connect continuity and rupture, without making the
        computational model a cognitive proof in itself.
  \item \textcite{martin2016creativeprocess} distinguishes self-judgment from
        consensus, measures the selective effect of feedback, and shows that judges
        prefer compositions produced at an experience level close to their own.
  \item \textcite{jones2009stimulating} describes computational feedback as a
        controlled distortion between repetition and estrangement, capable of opening
        new directions of exploration.
  \item \textcite{pachet2008machines} presents reflexive systems as machines
        favouring departure from oneself, and interesting objects as side-effects
        of interaction with an imperfect image of the subject.\par
  \item \textcite{aucouturier2002similarity} distinguishes the validity of a
        measure from its usefulness and locates an Aha effect in the contradiction
        between timbral similarity and textual expectation.
  \item \textcite{pachetroy2008hitsong} and \textcite{pachet2011hitsong}
        establish the limits of popularity prediction and reconstruct the target
        as a joint product of form, exposure, and social influence; the chapter
        also discusses artificially difficult canary trills.
  \item \textcite{lerch2011closedloop} shows, in a distinct protocol on female
        canary calls, that contingent interaction elicits more vocalisations than
        playback of the same stimuli.
  \item \textcite{pachet2006creativity} defines creativity as the situated feeling
        of producing something new and interesting, and shifts the unit of analysis
        to the human--machine pair.
  \item \textcite{pachet2006reflective} specifies scaffolding of complexity, the
        shift of attention toward the subject, and sustained interest in reflexive
        musical systems.
  \item \textcite{pachet2004cybernetic} and
        \textcite{pachet2006interactionsreflexives} explicitly shift the criterion
        from coherent material to an interesting or enjoyable interaction, whose
        quality may matter more than its product.
  \item \textcite{pachet2004childrenreflect} and
        \textcite{addessi2005experiments} observe young children learning implicit
        rules, negotiating mismatches and dead moments, and creatively reusing errors
        in dialogue with the Continuator.
  \item \textcite{addessi2006flowmachine} proposes a behavioural observation grid
        for flow and compares conditions with and without the system, within the
        limits of a small exploratory sample.
  \item \textcite{pachet2004flowmachines} describes attention, flow, and the
        dynamic mirror in interactions with the Continuator.
  \item \textcite{pachet2004reification} makes nourishing examples and the
        resistance of uses a condition for the fertility of models.
  \item \textcite{pachet2002continuatorinteraction} reports the Aha effect,
        intimate control, and the retention of musical regions judged interesting
        during interaction.
  \item \textcite{pachet2000melodie}, later republished in editorial form as
        \textcite{pachet2006clefs}, offers an initial objective model of
        interesting melodies whose relational limits the thesis examines.
  \item \textcite{pachet1999active} shifts musical access from isolated items to
        paths and variations that preserve semantic invariants.
  \item \textcite{pachet1999programs} constructs music programmes as a constrained
        compromise between repetition and surprise, local continuity and global
        variety.
  \item \textcite{pachet1999surprising} distinguishes raw unexpectedness from a
        surprise that listeners can reconstruct from acquired expectations and
        rules.
\end{itemize}
